Selling a money product is marketing on hard mode. The buyer is cautious. The stakes are personal. Every claim you make is checked by a compliance team before it ships. You cannot exaggerate, and you should not try.

That constraint turns out to be a gift. When you can't lean on hype, you're forced to get good at the things that actually work.

## Trust is a funnel stage

In most categories, marketers talk about awareness, consideration, conversion. In wealth and investing, there's a step hiding between consideration and conversion: *do I believe you with my money?*

You can't buy your way past it. It's earned through plain language, visible credentials, honest risk disclosure, and consistency over time. Once you start treating trust as a stage you can design for — rather than a vibe — the same thinking improves every considered purchase, from enterprise software to healthcare.

## The gap between signing up and actually starting

Money products have a brutal drop-off between "created an account" and "actually used it." Someone downloads an app, gets partway through verification, and vanishes.

Fixing that is rarely a marketing-campaign problem. It's a sequence problem: what the product asks for, in what order, and what messaging carries someone through the boring middle. Lifecycle and onboarding work — not more ads — is usually where the growth is hiding.

Every SaaS product with a trial-to-paid gap has the same shape of problem.

## Compliance makes you a better writer

When a lawyer reviews your copy, vague claims die first. What survives is specific, defensible, and clear. That's not a limitation on good marketing — it's a description of it.

I've come to think of regulated categories as training grounds. If you can make a complex financial product feel simple and trustworthy without overstating anything, a B2B SaaS positioning brief is not going to frighten you.

## Where this leaves me

I still love this category — the depth, the seriousness, the fact that getting it right genuinely helps people manage their money better. But the playbook it taught me isn't category-locked.

Complex product, cautious buyer, long consideration cycle, real consequences for choosing wrong? That's the same job, whatever the industry calls itself.
